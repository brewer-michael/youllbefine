🎛️ What Each Knob Does

Knob 1-4: Federal Loan Amounts
Federal Direct Loans have annual caps. For a dependent student: $5,500 (Yr 1), $6,500 (Yr 2), $7,500 (Yr 3 & 4). Max total: $31,000. Subsidized portion (where the government pays interest while in school) is capped at $3,500/$4,500/$5,500/$5,500. Always take subsidized first.
Knob 5: Endowment Help
You mentioned an endowment kicks in Year 2. This is free money that directly reduces what she needs to borrow. Every $1,000/yr in scholarship = $4,000 less debt over 4 years + saved interest.
Knob 6: PA State Grant
PHEAA's PA State Grant for PA residents at PA schools. Need-based, up to ~$4,000+/year. Requires FAFSA. This is a grant, not a loan.
Knob 7: Part-Time Job Income
Even $2,000/year applied directly to loan principal makes a big difference. $8,000 over 4 years = $8,000 less borrowed + ~$520 less in accrued interest. Work-study jobs on campus are ideal — they don't reduce financial aid eligibility.
Knob 8: PA Forward Private Loan Repayment Option
Only needed if there's a gap after grants + federal loans. The big choice:
• Full Deferral: No payments in school, but interest accrues and capitalizes (added to principal). Most expensive over the life of the loan.
• Partial Interest ($25/mo): Keeps interest from snowballing. Cheap way to save thousands.
• Interest Only: Principal stays flat. Best financial choice if she can afford it.
• Avoid entirely: The goal. Maximize grants, scholarships, and federal loans first.

How this works: She gets 26 paychecks/year. If she lives on roughly one paycheck per month (12 paychecks) and puts the other 14 entirely toward loans, that's an extra ~$13,468/year above her minimum payment. At that rate, even $25K in loans evaporate in under 2 years.
The 3rd-paycheck months: Most districts pay on a 26-pay schedule, which means 2 months/year have 3 paychecks. If she also throws the 3rd check at loans, that's another ~$1,418 x 2 = $2,836/year extra.
Real talk: Living on $1,800/mo means a roommate, cooking at home, and an older car. It's not glamorous for 2 years — but being completely debt-free at age 24 is worth it. After that, she still has 3 more years before Teacher Loan Forgiveness would even kick in. If she's already wiped the loans, that's $17,500 of profit she would have otherwise been chasing.

Payoff time uses actual amortization: extra payments reduce principal immediately, reducing future interest. Assumes she still makes minimum payments during the 6-month grace period, then goes aggressive.
